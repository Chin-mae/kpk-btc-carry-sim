# The four fund strategies, explained properly
Written 5 Nov 2026 for Chinmay (learning copy; mirrors the Monte Carlo spec's portfolio retarget). Every jargon term is defined at first use. Fund premise: a BTC-denominated fund — investors deposit BTC and judge performance in BTC (yield = "did my BTC stack grow?"), so every strategy below must end with profit converted back into BTC.

## Strategy 1 — BTC lending carry (the anchor sleeve)
**What it is in one line:** pledge BTC, borrow dollars against it, lend those dollars out at a higher rate than the loan costs, keep the difference, convert it back to BTC.

**Mechanics, step by step (worked example, $100 of BTC):**
1. Supply $100 of WBTC/cbBTC to Aave v3 Core as collateral (assets pledged as loan security).
2. Borrow USDC at the strategy's chosen LTV (loan-to-value: loan ÷ collateral). KPK's template chooses $70.20 per $100 (70.2% LTV) against a protocol ceiling (LLTV, liquidation LTV) of $85.
3. Deploy the borrowed USDC where it earns more than the loan costs: KPK's own USDC Yield vault and USDC Yield RWA vault (RWA = real-world asset; this vault's yield partly comes from tokenised offchain assets like Treasury bills and AAA loan bundles).
4. Earn the spread: deployment yield minus borrow cost. Spread × borrowed amount, recycled, is the gross engine; because the fund re-supplies and the profit compounds, KPK's realised figure at a 100bp spread (bp = basis point = 0.01%) is ≈2.36% per year in BTC terms at its template settings, before costs and bad paths.
5. The exit agent (an automated program with written rules, no human signer) watches the health factor (HF: cushion ratio; seizure starts at HF 1.0, KPK targets 1.21, full unwind below 1.13) and deleverages or fully unwinds on triggers: BTC −10% in 24h, spread negative for two 6-hour windows, wrapper depeg, venue freeze.
**Yield source:** other borrowers pay interest; part of it is the market's payment for using our BTC as security. No price prediction anywhere.
**What kills it:** BTC falls ~17.4% faster than the agent can deleverage at template LTV (liquidation: the protocol seizes and sells collateral, keeping a bonus for the liquidator); the spread inverts (borrow rate above deployment yield, e.g. when Aave utilisation crosses the kink — the 90% utilisation point past which borrow rates spike by design); the borrowed dollars get trapped at 100% utilisation in a destination vault; a wrapper depegs while the oracle still values it at par.
**Behaviour in hacks:** the 29 Oct 2026 freeze is this sleeve's home scenario — KPK's real fund exited in one block (12 seconds). The freeze scenario S3 asks the inverse: what if the frozen market is ours, for 12–72 hours, mid-crash.
**Simulation treatment:** deepest model in the engine: full Aave rate formula, own-size APY dilution on destination vaults, agent policy as code, liquidator agents.

## Strategy 2 — Basis and funding (the gated sleeve)
**What it is in one line:** own BTC and simultaneously short (bet against) the same amount of BTC in the futures market, so price moves cancel; collect the payments and price gaps that futures markets hand to the hedged side.

**Two variants, both used:**
(a) **Dated futures cash-and-carry.** A futures contract is an agreement to buy/sell BTC at a set price on a set date. Futures usually trade above spot (today's) price — that premium is the basis, and the above-spot state is called contango (the reverse, futures below spot, is backwardation). Example: spot $100,000, 3-month future $101,500 → basis 1.5% for the quarter. Buy spot, sell the future. At expiry (the contract's end date) the future's price must converge (meet) the spot price — that is a contract rule, not a hope. Whatever BTC does in between, the pair locks the $1,500 gap, minus fees and the cost of posting margin (the cash buffer an exchange demands while the trade is open; if the short leg loses on paper during a rally, margin must be topped up, and failing to top up gets the leg liquidated even though the combined trade is hedged — this liquidity risk is the variant's real danger, and why the fund holds a T-bill buffer).
(b) **Perpetual funding harvesting.** A perpetual future (perp) has no expiry; instead longs and shorts exchange a funding payment every 8 hours to keep the perp price near spot. When traders are mostly long (bullish), longs pay shorts. Holding spot BTC + a short perp collects that payment stream. Ethena's disclosed BTC funding income: 11.0% (2024) → 4.9% (2025) → 2.2% (2026) — the stream is real and currently thin. Funding can flip negative (shorts pay longs) for weeks in bearish regimes: the sleeve then pays to exist, which is why it is gated (below).
**The gate (the strategy's most important feature):** the sleeve runs only while its expected return exceeds T-bill yield + 150bp (1.5%). Below the hurdle, capital rotates to Strategy 4. Mid-2026 public CME basis ran ≈3%, below T-bills — a fund running this sleeve ungated in 2026 was working for less than a bank deposit with more risk. The gate, and a simulation that shows the sleeve OFF for most of 2026, is the honesty proof.
**What kills it:** funding flips negative for months; margin calls on the short leg during a violent rally drain the buffer; venue risk (the futures leg sits on an exchange or a DeFi perp protocol — an exchange hack hits this sleeve directly, exactly like the 29 Oct scenario); basis compresses to zero as competing capital crowds in.
**Simulation treatment:** medium depth: funding-rate series modelled from history with regimes; dated basis from CME-style curves; margin-call mechanics and buffer sizing are simulated explicitly, because the buffer size is a design decision the memo must recommend.

## Strategy 3 — Hedged concentrated-liquidity AMM basket (the job-ad sleeve)
**What it is in one line:** supply BTC and dollars to Uniswap trading pools inside chosen price ranges, earn traders' fees, and continuously cancel the position's price exposure with a futures hedge, so what remains is fee income minus hedging friction.

**Mechanics:**
1. An AMM (automated market maker) is a pool holding two assets where a formula sets the price; traders swap against the pool and pay a fee (e.g. 0.05–0.30% per trade depending on the pool's fee tier). Liquidity providers (LPs) supply the assets and split the fees (LP = liquidity provider).
2. Concentrated liquidity (Uniswap v3/v4): instead of spreading capital across all prices, the LP picks a range, say BTC $95k–$105k. Inside the range the capital works many times harder (fees per dollar far higher); outside it, it earns nothing.
3. The catch — the position changes itself. As BTC rises inside the range, the pool automatically sells your BTC for dollars (that is how traders buy from the pool); as BTC falls, it buys BTC with your dollars. You always end up holding more of whichever asset is losing. The resulting loss versus simply holding is impermanent loss (IL); its modern, precise name is LVR (loss-versus-rebalancing): the amount arbitrage traders systematically extract from LPs by trading against the pool's stale prices.
4. Delta and the hedge: delta is the position's price exposure (how many dollars of value change per $1 BTC move). An LP position's delta falls as price rises (it holds less BTC). Hedging = holding a short perp sized to the position's current delta, rebalanced as delta drifts (spec gate cadence: every 4h or on a 5% price move). Perfectly hedged, the sleeve's P&L ≈ fees − IL/LVR − hedge costs (funding paid or received on the hedge, rebalancing fees).
5. Entry/exit discipline (from the research evidence): across Uniswap v3 as a whole, LPs earned $199.3M of fees against $260.1M of IL — the average LP loses to simply holding. So the sleeve only enters pools where forecast fees ≥ 2× forecast IL, and exits below 1×. Forecasts use the pool's volume (trading activity feeding fees) and realised volatility (how violently price has been moving, which drives IL).
**Yield source:** traders pay for immediacy; the LP is a market maker (a firm that stands ready to buy and sell and earns the spread/fees). It is real work with real costs, not passive income.
**What kills it:** a fast one-directional move (IL explodes, hedge rebalance chases it), range exits leaving capital idle, fee drought when volumes migrate, and smart-contract risk in a newer venue (Robinhood Chain pools are young and thin — the largest SPY pool holds ~$3.15M, so this sleeve has a low capacity ceiling at $50M scale, which the memo must state).
**Simulation treatment:** medium depth: LP position mathematics are exact (the v3 formulas are closed-form), fee income modelled from volume series, IL/LVR computed per path, hedge rebalancing simulated at the gate cadence with costs.

## Strategy 4 — Tokenised T-bill sleeve (the brake and the buffer)
**What it is in one line:** idle dollars held in tokens backed by short-term US Treasury bills (instruments like BlackRock's BUIDL) — the fund's cash.
**Mechanics:** dollars are swapped into a token whose issuer holds T-bills (US government IOUs under 1 year) offchain; the token's value accrues the T-bill yield (≈4–5% across 2024–26, now the hurdle everything else must beat). Redemption (selling back to the issuer) settles T+0/T+1 (same or next business day); secondary-market sale is instant but at whatever price the market offers.
**Roles in the portfolio, all three are strategy, not leftovers:** (i) destination when Strategy 2's gate closes and when Strategy 1's carry inverts; (ii) the margin and redemption buffer — cash that meets investor withdrawals and futures margin top-ups without forcing another sleeve to unwind at fire-sale prices; (iii) the benchmark: any sleeve that cannot beat this after risk is destroying value, and the memo says so sleeve by sleeve.
**What kills it:** almost nothing market-side; the risks are issuer/custodian (a company holds the bills), redemption freezes in a crisis, and opportunity cost in a bull market. It is the only sleeve whose risk is counterparty paperwork rather than price.
**Simulation treatment:** simple series: T-bill rate from FRED, redemption-lag mechanics switched on in crisis regimes.

## How the four combine (the portfolio layer)
Starting allocation (a prior: a reasoned starting point the simulation tests and replaces, NOT a result): Carry 45%, Basis 20% (gated), AMM 15%, T-bill 20%. Provenance: descends from the 4 Nov brief's sleeve ranges, re-cut — carry largest (deepest evidence, no capacity problem), basis cut from 30–40% because its gate is closed most of 2026, AMM capped by pool depth (≈$7.5M is already aggressive at $50M fund size), T-bill 20% = the brief's ≥20% next-day-liquidity floor for redemptions and margin. Full reasoning: MONTE-CARLO-SPEC.md §11. The manager agent follows written rules: gates open/close sleeves by their hurdles; weights rebalance weekly or on trigger events; per-sleeve caps prevent any single venue from holding more than a set share; the T-bill buffer never falls below the level the margin/withdrawal simulation demands. The Monte Carlo's portfolio question: across 10,000 futures, which allocation rule set maximises median BTC yield for a fixed worst-5% loss budget, and by how much does diversification (sleeves failing on different days) actually reduce the tail versus the carry alone? That comparison — single-strategy fund vs multi-strategy fund, same futures, same seeds — is the memo's headline exhibit.
